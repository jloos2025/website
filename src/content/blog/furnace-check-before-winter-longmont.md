Most of the no-heat calls we take in January could have been caught in October. The failures that leave a house cold on the worst night of the year rarely happen without warning. They just happen without anybody looking.

Here is what you can check yourself before the first hard freeze, and where the line is between a homeowner job and a call to us.

## 1. Run the heat now, while it does not matter

This is the single most useful thing on the list, and almost nobody does it. Some warm afternoon in early fall, turn the thermostat up until the furnace fires, and let it run for fifteen or twenty minutes.

You are looking for whether it lights at all, whether it keeps running, and whether the air coming out actually gets warm. A furnace that fails this test in September is an inconvenience. The same furnace failing in January, on a night when every heating company in Boulder County is already booked, is a much worse day.

Expect a brief burning smell the first time it runs each season. That is dust burning off the heat exchanger and it should clear within fifteen minutes or so. A burning smell that does not clear is worth a call.

## 2. Change the filter, then look at the old one

A dirty filter is the most common cause of the service calls we get. It chokes airflow, which makes the furnace overheat and shut itself off before the house is warm. People read that as a broken furnace. It is usually a five dollar part.

For a standard one inch filter, change it every one to three months, sooner with pets. When you pull the old one, look at it. If it is grey and matted after a month, something else is going on, and it is worth mentioning when you call.

Put the new one in facing the right direction. There is an arrow on the frame and it points toward the furnace.

## 3. Make sure the furnace can breathe

Walk down to the furnace and look at what has accumulated around it since spring. Storage boxes, paint cans, and laundry piles stacked against the unit block the airflow it needs and, with a gas furnace, create a genuine hazard.

Give it clear space on all sides. While you are there, check that the supply and return vents upstairs are not blocked by furniture or rugs. Closing vents in unused rooms does not save money the way people assume. It raises pressure in the duct system and makes the furnace work against itself.

## 4. Check the exhaust outside

If you have a high efficiency furnace, it vents through white PVC pipe out the side of the house rather than up a chimney. Go outside and find it.

That pipe needs to stay clear. Snow drifts, leaves, mulch, and the occasional bird nest all block it, and a blocked exhaust shuts the furnace down on a safety switch. This is worth rechecking after any heavy snow, because a drift that buries the vent will stop your heat at exactly the moment you need it.

## 5. Test the thermostat and the safety alarms

Put fresh batteries in the thermostat if it takes them. A thermostat with dying batteries produces symptoms that look exactly like a failing furnace.

More importantly, test your carbon monoxide alarms and replace them if they are more than seven years old. Every gas appliance in the house is a reason to have working CO detection, and the start of heating season is the right time to confirm it.

## Where to stop and call

Some things are not homeowner territory. Call us, and do not keep running the system, if you notice any of this:

- The furnace lights and then shuts off repeatedly, over and over
- Banging, screeching, or a rumble that was not there last year
- A burning smell that does not clear after the first fifteen minutes
- Soot or scorch marks anywhere on or around the unit
- Your carbon monoxide alarm sounds, in which case leave the house first and call from outside

Those are symptoms of airflow, combustion, or safety problems, and the honest answer is that diagnosing them takes instruments and experience rather than a checklist.
